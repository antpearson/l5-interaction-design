Week 3 · in class and directed task

Researching and verifying your story

A quarter of Component 1 is research and verification. This is how those marks are won, and the handful of ways they are routinely thrown away.

Your piece claims things about the world. This is how you earn the right to.

Parts one and two are read in the Week 3 session, after the pitch. The rest is your directed task before Week 4. It takes about twenty minutes in all, and it maps directly onto the first Component 1 criterion, which is worth 25% of that component on its own.

What the criterion actually says

The criterion is “Research the story and its influences”. The top band asks for a story “researched in exceptional depth” where “every claim [is] verified against authoritative sources”, the research “demonstrably shapes the narrative arc”, and influences are “analysed with insight and their lessons put to work”. The narrative-arc phrase is the one students miss. It is not enough to have done research and to have made a piece. The marker needs to see the research changing the piece. The influences half is your key influences list: every piece you have read since Week 1, with a line on what you took from it.

One

What counts as a source

Not all evidence is equal, and at Level 5 you are expected to be able to tell the difference and to say why you trusted what you trusted.

Roughly in order of how much weight a claim can carry.
Kind Examples Use it for
Primary: the record itself Satellite imagery, census returns, minutes, court records, the dataset, the photograph, the person who was there. Anything load-bearing. This is what “authoritative” means.
Institutional NASA, USGS, ONS, Historic England, a named university department, a national library. Figures, dates, definitions. Usually citable as-is.
Serious journalism Reporting that names its own sources and can be checked. Narrative, context, quotations. Follow it back to its source where you can.
Specialist and local Local history societies, trade press, subject enthusiasts, archives with a named curator. Detail and colour nobody else has. Corroborate before relying on it.
Aggregated and unattributed Wikipedia, listicles, content farms, an AI summary, a caption on social media. Finding sources, never being one. Follow the footnote and cite what it points at.

The Wikipedia rule

Use it constantly. Cite it never. Wikipedia's value to you is the reference list at the bottom : that is somebody else's afternoon of source-hunting, given away free. Go and read the things it points at, and cite those. The same logic applies to anything an AI tool tells you: it is a lead, not a citation, and it is sometimes confidently wrong.

Two

Checking a claim

Take the sentences your piece cannot survive without: usually there are only five or six : and put each one through this.

  1. Who first said it? Follow the claim back until you reach somebody who was in a position to know. Most false things on the internet are true things that lost their context three retellings ago.

  2. Does a second, unrelated source agree? Unrelated is the important word. Six sites repeating one press release is one source wearing six hats.

  3. When was it true? Figures go stale. “The last remaining”, “the largest in Europe” and “currently closed” all have expiry dates. Write the date of the claim next to the claim.

  4. Who benefits from you believing it? Not cynicism, just bookkeeping. A company's own figure for its emissions is still usable; it just gets labelled as what it is.

  5. What would prove it wrong? If you cannot imagine what evidence would change your mind, you are writing an argument rather than a story, and the marker will feel it.

Checking a picture

Images travel further from their captions than text does. Before you build a section around a photograph, run a reverse image search on it and find the earliest version you can. You are looking for two things: whether it shows what the caption says it shows, and where it came from in the first place. A dramatic picture that turns out to be from a different decade and a different country will take a whole section of your piece down with it.

Three

Images you did not take

The brief permits sourced media “in reasonable measure” and requires it to be credited. Two separate questions hide in that sentence: are you allowed to use it, and have you said where it came from. You need a yes to both.

Are you allowed?

  • Public domain: no restriction. Most NASA and USGS imagery; a lot of very old photography.
  • Creative Commons: check which flavour. CC BY needs credit. CC BY-SA needs credit and obliges you to share alike. NC means non-commercial, which coursework generally is. ND forbids modification, which includes cropping.
  • All rights reserved: assume this by default when nothing says otherwise. Ask. Archives and small museums say yes to students more often than you would expect, and an email costs nothing.
  • Found on a search engine: not a licence. Where an image is displayed tells you nothing about who owns it.

Have you credited it?

Every piece needs a credits section, and every sourced item needs: what it is, who made it, where you got it, and under what terms. Build it as you go. Reconstructing it in January from forty files called image7.jpg is a genuinely miserable evening.

Make your own where you can

A photograph you took, a chart you built from a public dataset, a diagram you drew: no licence question, no credit anxiety, and it makes the piece unmistakably yours. Markers notice. If your subject is within reach of a bus, go and photograph it.

Four

Research involving people

If your story involves talking to anyone (an interview, a quotation, a photograph of someone identifiable), a few things apply, and they apply whether or not the person is a friend.

  • Say who you are, that it is university coursework, and that it will be published on the open web. All three, before they start talking.
  • Get their agreement in writing. A text message saying yes is fine; keep it.
  • Let them see how they are represented before it goes live, if the subject is personal.
  • Do not identify anyone who has not agreed to be identified, including in the background of photographs.
  • Anyone under 18, or anyone who might reasonably be considered vulnerable, means you talk to me first.

None of this is bureaucracy for its own sake. The piece goes on a public GitHub Pages URL that outlives the module, and that changes what you owe the people in it.

Five

Keeping a record

One list. Start it today. It is the difference between an afternoon and a weekend when you assemble Component 1, and it is direct evidence for a criterion worth a quarter of that component. Keep it as a text file in your project folder, sources.md, so every commit timestamps it; the story research section of your Component 1 PDF is this list, tidied.

sources.md: one entry per source, in the order you found them

## Aral Sea surface area, 1960 vs 2010
Claim I am using: the lake lost most of its surface area within fifty years.
Source: NASA Earth Observatory, World of Change: Shrinking Aral Sea
URL: https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/world-of-change/
Kind: institutional / primary imagery
Date checked: 12 Oct 2026
Corroborated by: (second source here)
What this changed about my piece: I had planned to open in 1960.
  The imagery does not start until 1972, so the opening moves.

That last line is the one that earns marks. “What this changed about my piece” is precisely the “research demonstrably shapes the narrative arc” the criterion asks for, and almost nobody writes it down. It also takes ten seconds.

Reference formatting for the written parts of your submission follows the usual Leeds Beckett guidance. See Skills for Learning if you need a refresher on Harvard. In-piece credits can be less formal, but they must be complete.

Six

The AI-use log

The brief's rule is short. AI tools are welcome for generating and exploring ideas, and for explaining an error or a technique, in the same way as asking a classmate. You are not allowed to use AI to write your code or the text of your story; submitting AI-written code or text as your own is academic misconduct. Generated assets are for occasional, declared use only, never passed off as a real photograph or record, and a piece that relies on them scores lower for design. Section 9 of the brief has the full wording.

For research, the rule that matters is in Part two: treat anything an AI tool tells you as unverified. It will produce a plausible date, a plausible figure and a plausible source, and the source will sometimes not exist. A claim that came from a chatbot is not sourced. It is a lead you still have to check against something real.

The log itself is ai-use-log.docx, in the root of your starter project, with a spare copy on the module site. Add a row on the day, every time you use an AI tool for anything on this module, then commit. A copy is uploaded with each component, even if it says you used none. Declaring use costs you nothing. Not declaring it is an academic honesty matter.

Before Week 4

  • sources.md started, with at least five entries.
  • credits.md started, with every image you have downloaded so far.
  • A row in ai-use-log.docx for any AI use so far, or nothing if there was none.
  • Your key influences list, with a line on what you took from each piece.
  • All of it committed to your repository, so it is timestamped.
