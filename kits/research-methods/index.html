Research methods

Researching and verifying your story

Your piece claims things about the world. This is how you earn the right to.

Read this before you write the story research and key influences sections of your Component 1 PDF. It takes about ten minutes.

What the criterion asks for

“Research the story and its influences” is worth 25% of Component 1. The top band wants every claim “verified against authoritative sources” and research that “demonstrably shapes the narrative arc”. That second phrase is the easiest to miss: I need to see your research changing the piece. The influences half is your key influences list, with a line on what you took from each.

One

What counts as a source

Not all evidence is equal. Be ready to say why you trusted what you trusted.

Roughly in order of how much weight a claim can carry.
Kind Examples Use it for
Primary: the record itself Satellite images, census returns, minutes, datasets, original photographs, people who were there. Anything your story depends on.
Institutional NASA, USGS, ONS, Historic England, a university department, a national library, the official rules or documentation for whatever you are explaining. Figures, dates, definitions.
Serious journalism Reporting that names its own sources. Context and quotations. Follow it back to its source where you can.
Specialist and local Local history societies, trade press, archives with a named curator. Detail nobody else has. Check it against a second source.
Aggregated and unattributed Wikipedia and other wikis, listicles, AI summaries, social media captions. Finding sources, never being one.

If little has been written about your subject, you make the record: talk to people who were there, photograph it, look for public records. Log each one like any other source, and read Part Four first.

The Wikipedia rule

Use it constantly. Cite it never. Its value is the reference list at the bottom: read what it points at, and cite that.

Two

Checking a claim

Take the five or six sentences your piece cannot survive without and put each one through this.

  1. Who first said it? Follow it back to somebody in a position to know. Most false things online are true things that lost their context.

  2. Does an unrelated source agree? Six sites repeating one press release is one source.

  3. When was it true? “The largest” and “currently closed” go out of date. Write the date next to the claim. Facts have versions too: say which edition, version or year you mean. A claim about a whole period needs dated evidence, not a general impression.

  4. Who benefits from you believing it? A company's own figure is still usable; label it as theirs.

  5. What would prove it wrong? If nothing could, you are writing an argument, not a story.

If your piece explains how something works, you can invent the example you walk the reader through, but never the facts it rests on. Cite the official source, and show your working for any numbers you calculate.

Checking a picture

Run a reverse image search and find the earliest copy. Check that it shows what the caption says, and where it first came from.

Three

Images you did not take

Two questions: are you allowed to publish it, and have you said where it came from? You need a yes to both, and that goes for video, audio, fonts and text as well as pictures. Your piece goes on a public website, and the classroom exceptions in copyright law do not cover publishing (section 9 of the brief).

Are you allowed?

  • Public domain: free to use. Most NASA and USGS imagery; a lot of very old photography; much of what museums release as open access.
  • Creative Commons: read which licence. BY needs a credit. SA means your version must be shared on the same terms. NC rules out commercial use. ND rules out changing it.
  • All rights reserved: assume this when nothing says otherwise. Ask the owner; an email costs nothing.
  • A company's own terms: some say what you may reuse and how. Read them. Permission for one thing (the text, say) does not cover another (the artwork).
  • Google Images, news sites, social media, ripped video or music: not a licence, and the brief rules them out.

Have you credited it?

Every sourced item needs what it is, who made it, where you got it, and the licence. Build the list as you go: you will not remember in January.

Make your own where you can

Your own photograph, a chart from a public dataset, a diagram you drew: no licence question, and the piece is unmistakably yours. If your subject is a bus ride away, go and photograph it.

Four

Research involving people

This applies if your story involves anyone (an interview, a quotation, an identifiable photograph), friends included. Your piece will be public and will outlive the module.

  • Before they start, say who you are, that it is coursework, and that it will be published online.
  • Get their agreement in writing. A text saying yes is fine; keep it.
  • If the subject is personal, let them see how you represent them before it goes live.
  • Do not identify anyone who has not agreed, including in the background of photographs.
  • Anyone under 18, or anyone who might be vulnerable: talk to me first.

Five

Keeping a record

Keep one list, sources.md, in your project folder, so every commit timestamps it. The story research section of your Component 1 PDF is this list, tidied.

sources.md: one entry per source, in the order you found them

## Aral Sea, 1960 to 2000
Claim I am using: by 2000 the lake was a fraction of its 1960 size.
Source: NASA Earth Observatory, World of Change: Shrinking Aral Sea
URL: https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/world-of-change/aral-sea/
Kind: institutional / primary imagery
Date checked: 12 Oct 2026
Corroborated by: (second source here)
What this changed about my piece: I had planned to open in 1960.
  The NASA images start in 2000, so the opening moves.

The last line is the one that earns marks. It is the “research demonstrably shapes the narrative arc” the criterion asks for, and it takes ten seconds to write.

The written parts of your PDF use Harvard referencing; see Skills for Learning for a refresher. Credits on your page can be less formal, but must be complete.

Six

The AI-use log

AI may help with ideas and explain an error. It must not write your code or the text of your story, and any generated asset must be declared (section 9 of the brief has the full rule). For research, treat what a chatbot tells you as a lead, never a source: it invents plausible dates, figures and references.

The log is ai-use-log.docx in your starter project, with a spare on the module site. Add a row on the day, every time you use AI for this module. Upload it with each component, even if it says you used none. Declaring costs you nothing; not declaring is an academic honesty matter.

Before you hand in Component 1

  • sources.md with an entry for every source you rely on, each with its “What this changed about my piece” line.
  • credits.md complete, with every image you did not make.
  • Your key influences list, with a line on what you took from each piece.
  • A row in ai-use-log.docx for every use of AI, or a line saying you used none.
  • All of it committed to your repository, so it is timestamped.
