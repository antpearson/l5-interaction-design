Week 4

Storyboarding motion

Beats, not screens. The six moves shown working, your beat sheet finished, a storyboard drawn one frame per beat, and the start of your moodboard. Then a rolling Component 1 clinic.

You have a topic, a pile of sources and a first beat sheet. Today it becomes a plan you could hand to somebody else.

The storyboard is the Component 1 item students under-invest in, because it feels like preparation rather than work. It is not preparation. It is a quarter of the component, and in Component 2 it is the plan your whole build is marked against. A beat you have drawn is a beat you can build in an hour. A beat you are inventing at the keyboard will eat an evening.

The order today is deliberate and it is the opposite of what feels natural. Write before you draw. Draw before you build. Every step back from the screen makes changes cheaper, and you will change your mind a lot.

Before we start

On the table: your topic sentence, five sources, twelve images or data points, your three-column beat sheet, and your key influences list. If you are missing the images, tell me in the first ten minutes. There is a version of today's session that works from a thinner pile, but I need to know who needs it.

What to have with you

Paper and a pen you can draw with. The storyboard kit on the module site prints onto A4 and gives you beat sheets and empty frames; print a few before the session. Figma is for later in the week, once the paper version has stopped changing.

Part one

Sources on the table

Ten minutes. Everyone puts their best picture or dataset on the projector for thirty seconds and says where it came from and whether they are allowed to use it. Two reasons: it is the fastest licence check in the world, and somebody else's archive is very often the one you needed.

Part two

Beats, not screens

Last week you wrote a beat sheet: one row per beat, three columns. A beat is one change of page state as the reader scrolls. The cover holds, a caption appears, a photo swaps, a map pins, a chart draws itself, a slider becomes available. The scroll distance between beats is the pacing.

The mistake to avoid today is storyboarding screens: drawing what the page looks like at a few points, as if it were a set of slides. A scrolling piece has no screens. It has beats, and the storyboard is one frame per beat, showing what changed and what the reader did to cause it. Two words worth keeping apart:

  • A section is a container: a chunk of the page with a heading, some text, usually a picture. It is what the HTML is made of.
  • A beat is what happens. A plain section usually holds one beat (it arrives). A scene, where a picture is held still while steps of text pass over it, holds several: one per step.

A piece of this scale is usually eight to fourteen beats. Fewer than six and there is not enough journey for the scroll to mean anything; more than about sixteen and you are asking for a reading commitment the web rarely gets. Two or three scenes in a whole piece is plenty; six is a piece that has stopped moving.

Part three · Screening

The menu of moves

In Week 2 you named six moves from the projector. Today you see each one built and working, in the browser, so you can choose from them with some idea of what they cost. Watching only: the code starts next week, and by then you will know which of these your story needs.

The six moves, as a menu. Most good pieces use three or four, not all six.
Move What the reader sees What it costs to build What it is for
Hold Something stays on screen while other things scroll past it. Cheap. You built it in Week 2 with no script at all. Keeping the subject in view while you talk about it.
Reveal Something arrives as the reader reaches it: fades up, slides in, draws itself. Cheap. The first thing you write in Week 5. Directing attention. Used on everything, it directs attention nowhere.
Swap The held picture changes as each step of text arrives. Moderate. A hold plus a change per step. Before and after. Then and now. The same place across forty years.
Scrub The scrollbar is wired directly to a change: a counter runs, a line draws, a map zooms. Scroll back and it runs backwards. Moderate to build, expensive to get feeling right. Needs scroll distance to breathe. Anything continuous. The engine of most change-over-time stories.
Layer Foreground and background move at different speeds. Cheap to add, easy to overdo, and the first thing to make people feel ill. Depth and atmosphere. Use once.
Respond The reader does something other than scroll, and the piece answers. Varies. A button is cheap; a slider is moderate; a map you can search is a project. The moment of genuine interaction the brief asks for. The other five are motion design.

For each demo, notice two things. What the reader does to trigger it, because that is what your storyboard has to say. And what is on the page before it moves, because a reader who has asked their device for less motion, or who is on a slow connection, sees that version. If the still version says nothing, the move is carrying meaning that the content should be carrying.

Choose from the menu, do not order everything

Against each beat on your sheet you are about to write which move it uses, if any. “None, it is just there” is a legitimate and often correct answer. Stillness is what makes the moving parts count.

Activity 1 · 25 minutes · on your own, then in threes

Finish your beat sheet

Two more columns, one row per beat.

  1. The move. Which of the six, or none. Be stingy. Three or four moves across the whole piece is normal.
  2. What the reader can do. “Scroll” for most rows. Say so explicitly rather than leaving it blank, so it is clear you decided.
  3. Find your moment. At least one row has a real entry in that column: the designed moment of genuine interaction. Circle it. If there is not one, that is the job for the next ten minutes, and the “If you get stuck” list has a way in.
  4. In threes, read each other's. One question: “what does the reader get from this move that they would not get from the still version?” If nobody can answer, cross the move out.

Break

Part four

What a storyboard frame contains

A film storyboard shows what is on screen. A motion storyboard for scroll has to show something harder: what is on screen, and how it responds to a reader who controls the timeline. One frame per beat, five things in each.

  1. The frame. What is on screen at this beat. Rough boxes and squiggle-text are fine; this is not an illustration exercise.

  2. What is held and what moves. Mark the held elements. This is the single most useful piece of information for whoever builds it, which is you in three weeks.

  3. The trigger. Where in the scroll does this happen? “When the heading is four-fifths of the way up the screen” is precise enough. Next week that sentence becomes one line of code.

  4. The change, and how it feels. What changes, and whether it is fast and settling or slow and even. Write the feeling in words.

  5. What the reader can do. Copied from the beat sheet. Nothing, in most frames.

The notation

Use this and your storyboard becomes readable by somebody else, which is the standard Component 2 is marked against: “so precisely a third party could build it”. Six marks. Draw them in the margin of your first page as a key.

Storyboard notation. Hand-drawn is expected; consistency is what matters.
Mark Means
Solid rectangle The viewport at this beat
Hatched or shaded element Held in place while the reader scrolls
Single arrow Moves once, in this direction, when triggered
Double-headed arrow on a bar Tied to the scrollbar; runs backwards when the reader scrolls back
Dotted line across the frame The trigger point: where in the viewport this fires
Circled hand or pointer The reader can act here

Activity 2 · 30 minutes · on your own, on paper

Storyboard your first six beats

Not the whole piece: the opening six beats, properly. Getting six right teaches you more than getting fourteen vague, and the rest is your directed task.

  1. One frame per beat, in reading order. Draw the frame contents first, roughly.
  2. Mark what is held. Mark what moves and in which direction.
  3. Draw the trigger line and write what it means in words.
  4. Write the feeling of each change in three or four words.
  5. Find your interaction moment. Circle it. If it is not in the first six beats, note where it will be.
  6. Under each frame, one line: why this change, at this moment, serves the story. Annotations that say what happens are worth a pass. Annotations that say why are worth a first.

Then swap and test it

Hand your six frames to somebody who does not know your topic and ask them to describe what happens as they scroll. Where they hesitate is where your annotation is missing, not where they are being slow.

Part five

Pacing: how much scroll a beat is worth

In film, time is time. Here, the reader holds the clock, and the only thing you control is how much scrolling a beat costs. That is your pacing instrument, and it is measured in screen heights. Starting points, to be corrected in the browser next week:

  • About one screen per reveal. Something arrives, the reader takes it in, the next thing arrives.
  • Two to three screens for a scrub. A change tied to the scrollbar needs distance to breathe; squeezed into one screen it flickers past.
  • One screen per step of a held scene. Four steps is four screens of scrolling. Draw that; it is longer than it feels on paper.
  • Vary it deliberately. Long, short, short, long reads like writing; all-long reads like homework.
  • Slow is not the same as important. A two-second fade does not make a fact weightier; it makes the reader wait.

Write your estimate in screen heights beside each frame. Next week you build a page with nothing in it but those heights, scroll it, and find out how wrong you were. That page is called the grey box, and it is the fastest pacing test there is.

The thirty-second test

Most readers will not scroll carefully. Take your beat sheet and imagine somebody flicking to the bottom in half a minute. What do they come away with? If the answer is “nothing”, your story is hiding inside your transitions. The strongest pieces work twice: once at a flick and once properly.

Part six

Moodboard and concept sketches

The storyboard says what happens. The moodboard says what it looks and feels like: imagery, type, colour, tone. It is a Component 1 item, it is marked under concept and visual direction, and it is the one most people leave until the night before, which shows.

You do not need much. One page, or one Figma frame, with:

  • Six to ten images that have the feel you are after. Your own archive material belongs here, but so do things that are nothing to do with your topic: a poster, a book cover, a piece from your key influences list. Credit each one.
  • Two typefaces, one for headings and one for reading, from Google Fonts, shown at the sizes you would use them.
  • Three to five colours, with the one you will use least marked as the accent.
  • Three words for the tone. “Quiet, forensic, cold” is a design brief. “Modern and clean” is not.

Then the concept sketches: two or three of your storyboard frames drawn again with the moodboard applied, so it is clear what the cover and one scene might actually look like. Paper or Figma. These are sketches, not finished designs; the finished design is Component 2's job, and you will change your mind about most of it.

Figma, and where the storyboard ends up

Paper is faster for the first pass and nobody will penalise you for it: photograph it and it counts for Component 1. But Component 2 asks for the storyboard in Figma, one frame per beat with notes on what changes and how, so the work has to end up there. Once your paper version has stopped changing, rebuild it: one Figma frame per beat, stacked in reading order, the notes as text beside each frame (trigger, change, how it feels, reader action), the frames named the way you will name your sections. Not Figma's prototype mode: it cannot do scroll triggers, and it is not what the brief asks for. Sharing your file and its history with me is taught later; you do not need to do it this week.

Rolling · about 20 minutes · the last part of the session

Component 1 clinic

Studio work carries on; I work round the room in turn. Come with something specific rather than “can you look at my thing”. You will get far more out of ten minutes.

What I can most usefully look at, in rough order of value:

  • Your beat sheet, for whether the order earns itself.
  • Your evidence, for whether the load-bearing claims are actually sourced.
  • Your interaction moment, for whether it is a real choice or a button that says “more”.
  • Anything you are worried is too ambitious. This is usually a scoping conversation and it usually ends well.

If you get stuck

I cannot draw

Nobody is marking your draughtsmanship. Boxes, arrows and squiggles for text are the professional standard for this kind of storyboard, because the point is the decisions, not the picture. If it blocks you, build the frames in Figma from rectangles.

Every beat wants to be a held scene

That is the standard Week 4 disease, and it comes from having just seen the demos. Pick the two moments in your story that most deserve to stop the reader, and make those scenes. Everything else is a plain section. Contrast is what makes a scene feel like an event.

I cannot find a real interaction moment

Look for a place where the reader would naturally want to compare, choose, or check themselves. Before and after. Guess before you are told. Pick which of three you want to follow. Find your own town on the map. The test is that their action changes what they see, and that a different reader could do it differently.

My storyboard has twenty-five beats

Half of them are the same beat. Ask of each one what the reader now knows that they did not know before it; if the answer repeats, merge them. If they all survive, your topic is too broad and it is not too late to narrow it, but talk to me this week rather than after the hand-in.

Should I storyboard for phone or desktop?

Phone first. It is the harder constraint and it is what most readers will use. A scene that works in a 390-pixel-wide column will nearly always work wide; the reverse is often false, and finding out in Week 10 is expensive.
