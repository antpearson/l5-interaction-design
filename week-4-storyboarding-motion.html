Week 4

Storyboarding motion

Your story becomes a stack of sections, a beat sheet and a drawn storyboard that says what moves, what holds still, and where the reader gets to act.

You have a topic and a pile of sources. Today it becomes a plan.

A motion storyboard is the deliverable that carries a quarter of Component 1 and most of the design mark in Component 2. It is also, quietly, the thing that decides whether Weeks 7 to 10 are calm or frantic — because a scene you have drawn is a scene you can build in an hour, and a scene you are inventing at the keyboard will eat an evening.

The order today is deliberate and it is the opposite of what feels natural. Write before you draw. Draw before you build. Every step backwards from the screen makes changes cheaper, and you will change your mind a lot.

What to have with you

Paper and a pen you can draw with, plus Figma if you want it. Both are fine and paper is usually faster for the first pass — the storyboard kit on the module site prints onto A4 and gives you beat sheets and empty frames. Print a few before the session.

Part one

The section is the unit

A scrolling narrative is not a page with things on it. It is a stack of sections, read in one order, each doing one job. That is the whole architecture, and getting it right on paper is most of the battle.

The test for a section is a single sentence:

The one-sentence test

“This section exists so the reader [understands / feels / notices] ______.”

If you need the word “and”, it is two sections. If you cannot finish the sentence at all, it is not a section — it is material you like, and it goes in the credits or the bin.

Two words that get used loosely and are worth separating today:

  • A section is a chunk of story: some text, usually a picture, one job. Most of your piece is sections.
  • A scene is a section where a visual is held still while several steps of text pass over it. It is more expensive to build and more powerful when it lands. Two or three in a whole piece is plenty; six is a piece that has stopped moving.

A piece of this scale is usually eight to fourteen sections. Fewer than six and there is not enough journey for the scroll to mean anything; more than about sixteen and you are asking for a reading commitment the web rarely gets.

Part two · Activity 1

The beat sheet

Before you draw anything, write the beats as plain text. Text is the cheapest thing to throw away, and you should expect to throw away half of it. Drawing first is how people end up defending a structure because they spent two hours on the boxes.

Five columns. One row per section.

A beat sheet. The worked example below is one possible opening for a change-over-time piece.
# What the reader sees What they now know What moves What they can do
1 Full-bleed satellite image, 1972. One line of type. Where we are. That this is a real place. Nothing. It is already there when they arrive. Scroll.
2 The same frame, 2010, replacing the first. Something enormous has happened here. Cross-fade, slow, scroll-linked. Scroll back and forth over it.
3 Text section. Two paragraphs, no image. Roughly when, and roughly why. Text rises in as it arrives. —
4 Scene: map held still, four captions pass over it. The sequence of decisions that caused it. Map pinned; a marker moves per step. —
5 Before/after slider on one location. The scale of it, at human distance. Nothing until they touch it. Drag the handle. The designed moment.

Look at the last two columns. “What moves” being empty is a legitimate and often correct answer — stillness is what makes the moving parts count. And “what they can do” needs a real entry at least once: that is the designed moment of genuine interaction the brief requires, and Component 1 asks you to have identified it now.

Activity 1 · 30 minutes · on your own, then in threes

Write your beat sheet

  1. Take the line you drew last week. Mark on it every point where something changes. Those marks are your sections.
  2. Fill in columns one to three for every section. Ignore movement entirely for now.
  3. Read it back as prose, out loud, skipping the columns. If it does not hold together as a story told badly, no amount of animation will save it.
  4. Now do columns four and five. Be stingy with column four.
  5. In threes: read each other's. The only question you ask is “why is this one after that one?”

The most common fix

Sections two and three usually want to swap, and the piece usually wants to start one section later than you wrote it. Openings are where people warm up. Cut the warm-up.

Part three

What a motion storyboard contains

A film storyboard shows what is on screen. A motion storyboard for scroll has to show something harder: what is on screen, and how it responds to a reader who controls the timeline. Five things per frame.

  1. The frame. What is on screen at this moment. Rough boxes and squiggle-text are fine; this is not an illustration exercise.

  2. What is fixed and what is moving. Mark the held elements. This is the single most useful piece of information for whoever builds it, which is you in three weeks.

  3. What triggers it. Where in the scroll does this happen? “When the heading is four-fifths up the screen” is precise enough, and it is exactly the start: 'top 80%' you met in Week 2.

  4. How it feels. Fast and settling, or slow and even? You have the vocabulary for this from Week 2: power2.out arrives and comes to rest, none is mechanical. Write the feeling in words; the easing name is a bonus.

  5. What the reader can do. Nothing, in most frames. Say so explicitly rather than leaving it blank, so it is clear you decided.

Part four

The notation

Use this and your storyboard becomes readable by somebody else, which is the standard Component 2 is marked against — “so precisely a third party could build it”. It is six marks. Draw them in the margin of your first page as a key.

Storyboard notation. Hand-drawn is expected; consistency is what matters.
Mark Means Becomes, in code
Solid rectangle The viewport at this moment —
Hatched or shaded element Held in place while the reader scrolls position: sticky, or a ScrollTrigger pin
Single arrow Moves once, in this direction, when triggered gsap.from() with a scrollTrigger
Double-headed arrow on a bar Tied to the scrollbar; reverses when the reader scrolls back scrub: true — Week 7
Dotted line across the frame The trigger point — where in the viewport this fires start: 'top 80%'
Circled hand or pointer The reader can act here An event listener — Week 9
Grey box in the corner What this frame looks like with reduced motion on gsap.matchMedia()

Activity 2 · 40 minutes · on your own

Storyboard your first three scenes

Not the whole piece — the opening three sections, properly. Getting three right teaches you more than getting twelve vague.

  1. One frame per section, on paper or in Figma. Draw the frame contents first, roughly.
  2. Mark what is held. Mark what moves and in which direction.
  3. Draw the trigger line and write what it means in words.
  4. Write the feeling of each movement in three or four words.
  5. Find your interaction moment. Circle it. If it is not in the first three sections, note where it will be.
  6. Put a grey box in the corner of any frame that moves, showing the still version.

Then swap and test it

Hand your three frames to somebody who does not know your topic and ask them to describe what happens as they scroll. Where they hesitate is where your annotation is missing, not where they are being slow.

Break · 15 minutes

Part five

Pacing — scroll distance is your timeline

In film, time is time. Here, the reader holds the clock, and the only thing you control is how much scrolling a beat costs. That is your pacing instrument, and it is measured in screen heights.

  • One idea per screen height is the baseline. Two ideas in one screen means neither lands.
  • A held scene needs roughly one screen height per step. Four steps is four screens of scrolling. Draw that; it is longer than it feels on paper.
  • Vary it deliberately. Three long scenes in a row is a slog whatever is in them. Long, short, short, long reads like writing; all-long reads like homework.
  • Fire slightly before the reader expects it. An element that animates after they have already read it looks broken. top 80% is a good default for exactly this reason.
  • Slow is not the same as important. A two-second fade does not make a fact weightier; it makes the reader wait. Most entrance movement wants to be well under a second.

The thirty-second test

Most readers will not scroll carefully. Take your beat sheet and imagine somebody flicking to the bottom in half a minute. What do they come away with? If the answer is “nothing”, your story is hiding inside your transitions. The strongest pieces work twice: once at a flick and once properly.

Part six

Draw the reduced-motion version now

You wrapped your Week 2 effects in matchMedia, so the mechanism is already there. What is not there yet is the design decision: for each moving thing, what does a reader who has asked for less motion get instead?

There are only three honest answers, and picking one per scene takes seconds now and hours in Week 10.

  1. It is simply already there. Fine for entrances. This is what gsap.from() buys you.

  2. It becomes a static equivalent. A scrubbed before-and-after becomes two pictures side by side with a caption. A moving counter becomes the final number.

  3. It becomes a control. The reader gets a button or a slider and moves it themselves. Often the best answer, because it converts motion into agency — which is what this module is about anyway.

A useful diagnostic

If the still version of a scene communicates nothing, the motion is carrying meaning that the content should be carrying. That is worth catching on paper. It is the difference between motion design and interaction design, and it is the distinction the whole brief turns on.

Part seven

Doing it in Figma

Paper is faster for the first pass and nobody will penalise you for it — photograph it and it counts. But Component 2 asks for a Figma storyboard and an interactive prototype of key transitions, so the work has to end up there eventually, and starting now saves a rebuild.

  • One frame per section, stacked in reading order on a single page. The stack is the piece.
  • A separate board per scene, showing each step as its own frame. This is how you work out whether four steps is three too many.
  • Annotations as text beside the frame, not inside it. Trigger, movement, feeling, reader action.
  • Name your frames the way you will name your sections in HTML. 01-cover, 02-then-and-now. Next week those names become your markup, and matching them is a genuine time-saver.
  • Share your file with me now, with edit history visible. The brief asks for Figma version history as process evidence, and it only works if it has been running all along.

You have a free Figma education plan as a student. If yours has lapsed, renew it at figma.com/education before next week rather than hitting a paywall mid-build.

Rolling · the last part of the session

Component 1 clinic

Studio work carries on; I work round the room in turn. Come with something specific rather than “can you look at my thing” — you will get far more out of ten minutes.

What I can most usefully look at, in rough order of value:

  • Your beat sheet, for whether the order earns itself.
  • Your evidence, for whether the load-bearing claims are actually sourced.
  • Your interaction moment, for whether it is a real choice or a button that says “more”.
  • Anything you are worried is too ambitious. This is usually a scoping conversation and it usually ends well.

Directed task · before Week 5

Finish the storyboard, draft the document

Bring to Week 5

  • A complete beat sheet for the whole piece, not just the opening.
  • Storyboard frames for every section, annotated with the notation above. Rough is fine; complete is not optional.
  • Your designed interaction moment, drawn and annotated properly. This is a named criterion and it is the one most often left as a sentence.
  • A draft of your Component 1 document — story research, audience and intent, concept, interaction intent. Draft, not final. Next week is for fixing it.
  • Your laptop. Week 5 turns the storyboard into HTML sections, and you will want your own project open.

A note on effort distribution: the storyboard is worth 25% of Component 1 by itself and it is the part students under-invest in, because it feels like preparation rather than work. It is not preparation. It is the deliverable.

If you get stuck

I cannot draw

Nobody is marking your draughtsmanship. Boxes, arrows and squiggles for text are the professional standard for this kind of storyboard, because the point is the decisions, not the picture. If it genuinely blocks you, build the frames in Figma from rectangles.

Every section wants to be a pinned scene

That is the standard Week 4 disease, and it comes from having just learned pinning. Pick the two moments in your story that most deserve to stop the reader, and make those scenes. Everything else is a section. Contrast is what makes a scene feel like an event.

I cannot find a real interaction moment

Look for a place where the reader would naturally want to compare, choose, or check themselves. Before and after. Guess before you are told. Pick which of three you want to follow. Find your own town on the map. The test is that their action changes what they see, and that a different reader could do it differently.

My storyboard has twenty-five sections

Half of them are the same section. Group them by the one-sentence test and the duplicates fall out. If they genuinely all survive, your topic is too broad and it is not too late to narrow it — but talk to me this week rather than after the hand-in.

Should I storyboard for phone or desktop?

Phone first. It is the harder constraint and it is what most readers will use. A scene that works in a 390-pixel-wide column will nearly always work wide; the reverse is often false, and finding out in Week 10 is expensive.
