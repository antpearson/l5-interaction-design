Week 3

Finding your story

Four shapes a scrolling story can take, an archive round, and the pitch gate before the break. Then the research starts, and your story gets its first beat sheet. Nobody leaves today without a topic.

You have a page, a scene that holds still, and a sheet of thirty ideas. Today one of them becomes your story.

Everything from here is built on what you choose this afternoon. A strong topic makes Weeks 7 to 12 feel like craft. A weak one makes them feel like filling space. So the pitch comes early, before the break, and the second half of the session is spent on the thing you have just chosen rather than on choosing it.

This is also the part students find hardest, and that is worth saying out loud. Making a page move is a solvable problem with a right answer. Deciding what the page is about has no right answer, and the discomfort of that is not a sign you are doing it wrong.

Before we start

Bring your ideas sheet, your watch-list notes, and the three candidates you ran through the five tests. If you have nothing written, say so at the start rather than at the end. Part three has an archive round for exactly that, but only if I know who needs it.

From the assignment brief

What Component 1 asks for

One PDF, uploaded to MyBeckett by Friday 30 October, 12 noon, with your AI-use log as a separate file. Six things, in this order. Today starts four of them.

  1. Story research: your verified story or topic, with credible sources showing what is true and how you know starts today
  2. Key influences: the interactive narratives and other work that have shaped your thinking, and what you are taking from each started last week
  3. Audience and intent: who the piece is for and what you want them to understand or feel the pitch
  4. Your concept: premise and experience arc. What does your reader encounter, understand and do from the top of the page to the end? the beat sheet
  5. Moodboard and concept sketches: the visual direction of the piece (imagery, type, colour, tone) and early sketches of how it might look
  6. Interaction intent: a motion storyboard or annotated sketch of the scroll sections showing pacing beats, key transitions and at least one designed moment of genuine interaction

Four marking criteria, 25% each: research and influences; audience and intent; concept and visual direction; interaction intent. The wording above is the brief's own. If this page and the brief on MyBeckett ever disagree, the brief wins.

What to have open

Nothing technical before the break: your printed ideas kit or a notebook, and the archive links from Part three. After the break, your laptop and a document for your sources.

Part one · Debrief

What you saw

In pairs, ten minutes. Compare your watch-list notes. For each piece you both read: which of the six moves did it use, did the reader ever get to do anything but scroll, and what would you steal? Where you disagree about which move something was, say so; the argument is the useful bit.

Then a few of you tell the room the one thing you would steal. Write down anything somebody else says that you wish you had noticed. All of this goes in your key influences list, which is a Component 1 item in its own right, not a warm-up.

Originality lives in the treatment, not the topic

This comes up every year, so it is worth quoting the brief's principle back at you. Glaciers have been done. The high street has been done. It does not matter. A subject covered a hundred times can still make an outstanding piece if the telling is yours, and a topic nobody has touched can still make a dull one. Do not hunt for virgin territory. Find an angle you can defend.

Part two

Four shapes a scroll story can take

Before you choose a topic it helps to know what the medium is good at. Scroll is a single, one-dimensional input: forwards, backwards, at a speed the reader controls. That sounds limiting. It is a precise instrument, because it maps cleanly onto four narrative shapes.

The four shapes. Most pieces are mainly one, with a section borrowed from another.
Shape What the scroll is doing The reader's question Where it goes wrong
Change over time Scroll position stands for time. Down is later. “What happened to it?” The change is real but invisible: nothing to show.
Linear reveal Scroll controls the order facts arrive in. Down is the next thing you are allowed to know. “What happens next?” The order is arbitrary, so withholding feels like a trick.
Spatial journey Scroll stands for movement through a place. Down is further along, deeper in, higher up. “Where am I now?” You need a map or a consistent view, or the reader gets lost.
Layered explainer Scroll builds understanding. Down is one more layer on the same object. “How does it work?” Each layer needs the last one, so a reader who skims gets nothing.

Notice what all four have in common: down means something. That is the test of whether your idea belongs in this medium at all. If a reader could shuffle your sections into a different order and lose nothing, you have written an article with pictures, and the scroll is decoration.

Activity 1 · 15 minutes · in pairs

Name the shape, and draw the line

  1. Take one piece from the watch-list that you both read. Name its shape from the table. Argue if you disagree.
  2. Draw its line, Vonnegut-style. One axis is scroll position, top to bottom. The other is whatever the piece is tracking: how much ice, how much you know, how bad it is getting, how close you are.
  3. Mark the point where you were most gripped, and the point where you nearly stopped scrolling.
  4. Now draw the line for each of your own three candidates. If one of them will not turn into a line, that is diagnostic. It may be an article.

Keep these drawings

The line you draw for the topic you choose becomes your beat sheet after the break, and your storyboard next week. It is not a warm-up exercise; it is the first draft of your concept.

Part three

The five tests, and an archive to raid

You ran these at home on your five stars. Now run them on your three candidates with somebody else looking, because the answers you give out loud are more honest than the ones you write alone.

  1. Is there a change? Something has to be different at the bottom of the page from the top: in the world, or in the reader's head.

  2. Can you see it? This is a visual medium and you are not an illustrator with a spare fortnight. If you have not yet found five images or a dataset you are allowed to use, the archive round below is for that.

  3. Can you check it? Every claim in the finished piece needs a source you could show me. A quarter of the Component 1 mark is research. If your topic lives entirely in forum posts and half-memories, it will not survive that criterion.

  4. Is there a moment where the reader does something? The brief requires at least one designed moment of genuine interaction. You do not need to have designed it yet, but you should be able to point at a place in the story where letting the reader act would mean something.

  5. Do you care? Unsentimental version: you will look at this thing for eleven more weeks, in evenings, when it is not working. Mild interest evaporates around Week 8.

How to read your own results

Failing one test is a normal, fixable problem, usually solved by narrowing the topic rather than swapping it. Failing two means bring it to me at the pitch. Failing three means it is a different topic, and you have saved yourself six weeks by finding out today.

Activity 2 · 20 minutes · on your own

Raid an archive

Two jobs, depending on what you walked in with. If you have three candidates, this is where you find out whether the pictures exist: pick the archive most likely to hold your material and come back with five things you would be allowed to use, or with the knowledge that they are not there. If you arrived with nothing, pick one archive and go looking for something that is not the same as it used to be. Write down five, however thin. That is your three candidates, plus two spare.

  • World of Change NASA Earth Observatory · free to use with credit

    Around thirty places photographed repeatedly for decades: glaciers, lakes, cities, ice shelves. The single richest source in this list for a change-over-time piece.

  • Earthshots USGS EROS

    Landsat imagery of environmental change, paired with plain-English explanations of what you are looking at.

  • Side-by-side maps National Library of Scotland · covers all of Britain

    Ordnance Survey maps from the 1890s onwards beside a modern map of the same spot. Put your own street in and something will surprise you. There is a swipe version too.

  • Aerial Photo Explorer Historic England

    Over 540,000 digitised aerial photographs of England, searchable by place and by year flown. Wartime RAF and USAAF coverage through to recent decades.

  • Leodis Leeds Libraries

    A photographic archive of Leeds. Local, specific, and largely unmined by anyone making things for the web.

  • Our World in Data CC BY · reusable with credit

    Fourteen thousand charts across 126 topics, all sourced and all reusable. If your change is a number rather than a picture, start here.

On sensitive subjects

Some of the strongest work on this module comes from close to home: illness, migration, bereavement, a place that has been let down. That is welcome, and it is not off-limits. But if a topic involves real, identifiable people, talk to me at the pitch before you commit. There are things worth thinking about first: consent, whether they can say no once it is published, and whether you want your own experience marked out of a hundred by January. Sometimes the answer is yes and the work is remarkable. Sometimes the honest answer is that this one is not for coursework.

Activity 3 · 10 minutes · on your own

Cut to one

  1. Three candidates, five tests each, answers written down. Cross out anything that fails three.
  2. Of what is left, pick the one with the most pictures. At this level, available visual material is the difference between a piece you can finish and one you cannot.
  3. Write the pitch sentence below for it. If you cannot fill in the blanks, that tells you which test it really failed.

Not forever: you can change it up to the end of Week 4 by talking to me. But choose one now, because a decision made today gets a week of thinking that a decision made next Tuesday does not.

Part four · Pitch gate

The pitch

Ninety seconds each, to me, at the front, while everyone else carries on with the archive round. This carries no marks. It is a gate, not an assessment: the point is that nobody goes to the break still deciding.

Fill in this sentence and say it out loud:

The pitch

“My piece is about [what], for [who], so that they understand or feel [what]. It works as a scroll because [which shape, and why down means something]. My evidence so far is [sources]. Somewhere around [roughly where], I think the reader should get to [do something].”

Then one line on what worries you about it. That line is the most useful thing you will say, and it is usually the thing I can fix on the spot.

Notice that the second and third blanks are a Component 1 item in miniature: audience and intent is who it is for and what you want them to understand or feel. Write the sentence down after you say it. It is the first line of that section.

What I am listening for, in order:

  • Does down mean something? If the sections could be shuffled, we work on that first.
  • Is it narrow enough? Nine pitches in ten are too big. “Climate change” is not a topic; one glacier, one lake, one street is.
  • Does the evidence exist? Not gathered yet: existing. Have you seen it with your own eyes?
  • Is there a place for the reader to act? Rough is fine. “Somewhere in the middle” is fine.

Three outcomes, all of them normal

Agreed: go and start the research. Agreed, narrower: the most common outcome by a distance; you leave with the same subject and a smaller frame round it. Come back to me: usually an evidence problem, and usually resolved in a ten-minute conversation after the break. Nobody is sent away with nothing.

Break

Part five

Start the research

Laptops open. You have a topic, so the rest of today is spent on it. Twenty minutes here, then the beat sheet.

Open the research methods tutorial and read the first two parts: what counts as a source at this level, and how to check a claim. The rest is your directed task. Then start your source list, in whatever document your Component 1 will be written in, and get the first three sources into it properly: who, what, when, where you found it, and one line on what it tells you. Not three open tabs.

While you are in that document, make the second heading Key influences and move your watch-list notes under it. Each entry is a piece, and a line on what you are taking from it: a structure, a move, a way of pacing, a tone. It is a Component 1 item and it is the easiest of the six to do well, because you have been doing it since Week 1.

One question to answer before you leave

What is the single claim your piece depends on, and where will you get it from? If the answer is “Wikipedia”, follow its references to wherever the fact came from and record that instead.

Part six · Activity 4

Beats, and your first beat sheet

The brief uses one word for the unit your piece is made of, and from now on so does everyone in this room.

A beat

One thing that happens as the reader scrolls: a moment where the page changes state. The cover holds, a caption appears, a photo swaps from 1980 to 2020, a map pins in place, a chart draws itself, a slider becomes available. Each of those is a beat. Your piece is a sequence of them, and the scroll distance between them is the pacing.

Your line from Activity 1 is already a beat sheet in disguise: every place the line bends is a beat. Today you write them down as plain text, three columns, no movement yet. Text is the cheapest thing to throw away, and you should expect to throw away half of it. Next week the sheet gets its movement columns and becomes the storyboard.

A first beat sheet: one row per beat, three columns. The worked example is one possible opening for a change-over-time piece.
# What the reader sees What they now know What changed to get here
1 Full-screen satellite image, 1972. One line of type. Where we are. That this is a real place. Nothing. It is there when they arrive.
2 The same frame, 2010. Something enormous has happened here. The picture swapped.
3 Two paragraphs, no image. Roughly when, and roughly why. Text arrived.
4 A map, held still. First of four captions over it. The first decision that caused it. The map pinned and a marker appeared.
5 Before and after, one location, with a handle. The scale of it, at human distance. Nothing, until they drag it.

Activity 4 · 30 minutes · on your own, then in threes

Write your first beat sheet

  1. Take the line you drew for your chosen topic. Mark every point where something changes. Those marks are your beats. Eight to fourteen is usual; fewer than six and there is not enough journey for the scroll to mean anything.
  2. One row per beat. Fill in the three columns for every row, in order, top to bottom.
  3. Read it back as prose, out loud, skipping the columns. If it does not hold together as a story told badly, no amount of movement will save it.
  4. Mark the row where the reader could act. One is enough. If there is not one, that is the first thing to fix next week.
  5. In threes: read each other's. The only question you ask is “why is this one after that one?”

The most common fix

Beats two and three usually want to swap, and the piece usually wants to start one beat later than you wrote it. Openings are where people warm up. Cut the warm-up.

Directed task · before Week 4

Gather your sources and your pictures

Next week you storyboard. You cannot storyboard a story you have not researched, so this week's task is the research, and Week 4 visibly depends on it.

Finish the research methods tutorial: how to credit images you did not take, research involving people, and how to keep a source list that still makes sense in January. It exists because a quarter of your Component 1 mark is research, and every year somebody loses those marks for reasons that are entirely avoidable.

Bring to Week 4

  • Your topic in one sentence, in the pitch format above, written down.
  • At least five credible sources, recorded properly in your source list.
  • Twelve images or data points you are allowed to use, with where each came from. Rough and unedited is fine. Twelve sounds like a lot; it is roughly one per beat, and you will discard half.
  • Your beat sheet, complete for the whole piece, three columns, redrawn now you know more than you did today.
  • Your key influences list with at least three entries and a line on each.
  • One thing that surprised you while researching. These are usually where the piece really starts.
  • Figma, working. Sign in at figma.com/education and check your free education plan is active. If it has lapsed, renew it now, not during a session.

If your topic falls apart during the week, because the pictures do not exist or the story turns out not to be true, email me. Do not wait until next Tuesday to say so. A topic that collapses in Week 3 costs you nothing. One that collapses in Week 8 costs you a grade.

If you get stuck

I genuinely have no ideas

You have ideas; you have a filter set too high. Go back to round one of last week's sheet and write five things you know about, then show the list to somebody else and watch which one they ask a question about. That is the one. The filter you are applying, “is this interesting enough, has this been done”, belongs at the end of the process, not the start.

I have too many and cannot choose

Run the five tests honestly and most of them fall away. If two survive equally, pick the one with more pictures. At this level, available visual material is the difference between a piece you can finish and one you cannot.

Someone has already made my piece

Good. That means the story works and the material exists. Read theirs properly, write down the three decisions you would make differently, and make yours. Every piece on the watch-list has a dozen predecessors. Just do not copy the structure beat for beat, and do put it in your key influences if it shaped your thinking.

My topic is interesting but nothing changes

Then it wants to be a layered explainer rather than a change-over-time piece. The change becomes the change in the reader's understanding. That is a legitimate shape and the brief covers it explicitly, but it puts more weight on the reveal order, so plan for that in Week 4.

I cannot find images I am allowed to use

Check the licence before you fall in love with a picture. NASA and USGS material is generally free to reuse with credit; Our World in Data is CC BY. Museum and library archives vary enormously and many allow educational use if you ask. If a picture is not licensable, you can often photograph, draw, chart or reconstruct the same thing yourself, and the piece is usually better for it. The research methods tutorial covers this properly.

I want to change my topic after today

Allowed, up to the end of Week 4, by talking to me first. After that you are storyboarding and the cost of switching gets steep. Changing once is normal; changing three times is a different problem and worth a conversation.
