Week 3

Finding your story

Four shapes a scrolling story can take, a room full of archives to raid, and the pitch gate. Nobody leaves today without a topic.

You have made something move. Now you need something to say.

This is the week that decides your mark. Not because it is assessed — the pitch carries no marks at all — but because everything from here is built on whatever you choose this afternoon. A strong topic makes Weeks 7 to 12 feel like craft. A weak one makes them feel like filling space.

It is also the part students find hardest, and that is worth saying out loud. Making a page move is a solvable problem with a right answer. Deciding what the page is about has no right answer, and the discomfort of that is not a sign you are doing it wrong.

Before we start

Bring your watch-list notes and the two or three topic ideas you were asked to write down. If you have nothing written, say so at the start rather than at the end — the workshop is designed to work from a blank page, but only if I know who is starting from one.

What to have open

Nothing technical today. A notebook or a blank document, the archive links from Part four, and your phone for photographing the wall at the end. Laptops stay shut for the first hour — this is deliberate.

Part one

Why the topic is the hard part

A template platform will build you a static site in an afternoon, and an AI tool will fill it with competent prose. What neither can do is know which story is worth telling, to whom, and why now. That judgement is the thing this course is actually selling, and this module puts it at the centre rather than treating it as the wrapper around some code.

So the brief is open on purpose. It asks for a true story or explainer — something that has changed, developed or depleted over time, or a subject the reader comes to understand step by step. Beyond that, the choice is yours.

Originality lives in the treatment, not the topic

This is worth quoting from the brief because it comes up every year. Glaciers have been done. Coral has been done. The high street has been done. It does not matter. A subject covered a hundred times can still make an outstanding piece if the telling is yours — and a topic nobody has touched can still make a dull one. Do not spend three weeks hunting for virgin territory. Spend them finding an angle you can defend.

Two routes to a topic exist and they are worth naming, because students tend to assume the second is somehow worth fewer marks. It is not.

  • The archive route. Start with material that already exists — a satellite series, a photographic collection, a dataset — and find the story sitting in it. Lower risk, because you know from day one that the pictures exist.
  • The knowledge route. Start from something you already know: your town, your old job, a family history, a subculture you are inside. Higher risk on sourcing, much higher ceiling on voice.

Most strong pieces end up being one crossed with the other. You bring the angle; the archive brings the evidence.

Part two

Four shapes a scroll story can take

Before you choose a topic it helps to know what the medium is good at. Scroll is a single, one-dimensional input: forwards, backwards, at a speed the reader controls. That sounds limiting. It is actually a precise instrument, because it maps cleanly onto four narrative shapes.

The four shapes. Most pieces are mainly one, with a section borrowed from another.
Shape What the scroll is doing The reader's question Where it goes wrong
Change over time Scroll position stands for time. Down is later. “What happened to it?” The change is real but invisible — nothing to show.
Linear reveal Scroll controls the order facts arrive in. Down is the next thing you are allowed to know. “What happens next?” The order is arbitrary, so withholding feels like a trick.
Spatial journey Scroll stands for movement through a place. Down is further along, deeper in, higher up. “Where am I now?” You need a map or a consistent view, or the reader gets lost.
Layered explainer Scroll builds understanding. Down is one more layer on the same object. “How does it work?” Each layer needs the last one, so a reader who skims gets nothing.

Notice what all four have in common: down means something. That is the test of whether your idea belongs in this medium at all. If a reader could shuffle your sections into a different order and lose nothing, you have written an article with pictures, and the scroll is decoration.

Activity 1 · 25 minutes · in pairs

Name the shape, and draw the line

Take three pieces from last week's watch-list — ideally ones you both read.

  1. Name its shape from the table above. Argue about it if you disagree; the arguments are where the learning is.
  2. Draw its line, Vonnegut-style. One axis is scroll position, top to bottom. The other is whatever the piece is actually tracking: how much ice, how much you know, how bad it is getting, how close you are.
  3. Mark on your line the point where you were most gripped. Then mark where you nearly stopped scrolling.
  4. Now draw the line for your own best idea so far. If you cannot draw a line, that is diagnostic, and we will talk about why.

Keep these drawings

The line you draw for your own piece becomes the beat sheet in Week 4 and the section structure in Week 5. It is not a warm-up exercise; it is the first draft of your storyboard.

Part three

The five tests

Ideas are cheap. Ideas that survive twelve weeks of building are not. Run every candidate through these five before you commit to it, and again next week when you have done some digging.

  1. Is there a change? Something has to be different at the bottom of the page from the top — in the world, or in the reader's head. “A tour of Leeds Corn Exchange” has no change. “How the Corn Exchange survived being nearly demolished twice” does.

  2. Can you see it? This is a visual medium and you are not an illustrator with a spare fortnight. Before you commit, find five images or a dataset you are actually allowed to use. If the change is real but invisible, you are signing up to invent every picture yourself.

  3. Can you check it? Every claim in the finished piece needs a source you could show me. A quarter of the Component 1 mark is research and verification. If your topic lives entirely in forum posts and half-memories, it will not survive that criterion.

  4. Is there a moment where the reader does something? The brief requires at least one designed moment of genuine interaction. You do not need to have designed it yet, but you should be able to point at a place in the story where letting the reader act would mean something. If you cannot, the topic may be fighting the medium.

  5. Do you care? Unsentimental version: you will look at this thing for eleven more weeks, in evenings, when it is not working. Mild interest evaporates around Week 8.

How to read your own results

Failing one test is a normal, fixable problem — usually solved by narrowing the topic rather than swapping it. Failing two means bring it to me before you commit. Failing three means it is a different topic and you have saved yourself six weeks by finding out today.

Break · 15 minutes

Part four · Activity 2

Generating ideas — three rounds

Three rounds, run against the clock, in this order. The order matters: we start outside your head and work inwards, because starting inwards produces the four ideas you already had when you walked in.

One rule for all three rounds: write everything down and judge nothing. Quantity now, quality later. If you find yourself deciding an idea is bad, you are in the wrong round.

Round A — Raid an archive

15 minutes · on your own

Pick one archive below and go looking. You are not looking for a story yet. You are looking for something that is not the same as it used to be. Write down five, however thin.

  • World of Change NASA Earth Observatory · free to use with credit

    Around thirty places photographed repeatedly for decades — glaciers, lakes, cities, ice shelves. The single richest source in this list for a change-over-time piece.
  • Earthshots USGS EROS

    Landsat imagery of environmental change, paired with plain-English explanations of what you are looking at.
  • Side-by-side maps National Library of Scotland · covers all of Britain

    Ordnance Survey maps from the 1890s onwards beside a modern map of the same spot. Put your own street in and something will surprise you. There is a swipe version too.
  • Aerial Photo Explorer Historic England

    Over 540,000 digitised aerial photographs of England, searchable by place and by year flown. Wartime RAF and USAAF coverage through to recent decades.
  • Leodis Leeds Libraries

    A photographic archive of Leeds. Local, specific, and largely unmined by anyone making things for the web.
  • Our World in Data CC BY — reusable with credit

    Fourteen thousand charts across 126 topics, all sourced and all reusable. If your change is a number rather than a picture, start here.

Round B — Prompts

15 minutes · on your own, then swap

Finish these sentences. Do not think hard. Write the first thing, then the second thing, then the stupid thing. Three answers each, minimum.

  • Something that used to be everywhere and now is not.
  • A building near me that has had at least three different lives.
  • A number that has gone one direction for fifty years.
  • Something everyone thinks they understand and almost nobody does.
  • A job that has nearly disappeared in my lifetime.
  • Something my grandparents would not recognise about where I grew up.
  • A thing I have to explain every time it comes up.
  • Somewhere I can walk to that has a story attached.
  • Something that got better, quietly, and nobody made a fuss.
  • A decision that gets made for people without them noticing.

Then swap sheets with the person next to you and add one idea to three of their answers. Other people's half-formed thoughts are unreasonably productive.

Round C — What you already know

10 minutes · on your own

List five things you know more about than the average person in this room. Any five. A job you have done, a place you have lived, an illness in the family, a game, a scene, a sport, a qualification, a hobby your friends find baffling.

Then, for each one, write the sentence a stranger would need to hear to understand why it matters. That sentence is very often the piece.

On sensitive subjects

Some of the strongest work on this module comes from close to home — illness, migration, bereavement, addiction, a place that has been let down. That is welcome, and it is not off-limits. But if a topic involves real, identifiable people, come and talk to me before you commit to it. There are things worth thinking about first: consent, whether they can say no once it is published, and whether you want your own experience marked out of a hundred by January. Sometimes the answer is yes and the work is remarkable. Sometimes the honest answer is that this one is not for coursework.

Part five · Activity 3

Cutting twenty ideas down to one

25 minutes · whole room, then on your own

Everything goes on the wall. One idea per sticky note, written so someone else can read it from a metre away. No names on them.

  1. Everyone reads everything. Ten minutes, standing up, no talking. You are allowed to steal. Genuinely — if someone else's discarded idea is the one you want, take it. Two people can make completely different pieces from the same subject, and often do.

  2. Three dots each. Put a dot on the three you would most want to read. Not the three you think are worthiest. The three you would actually click.

  3. Take back three of your own — dots or no dots. The dots are information, not a verdict. An idea nobody else understood at a metre's distance may still be the right one; it just told you something about how you will need to pitch it.

  4. Run the five tests on each of your three. Write the answers down. Cross out anything that fails three.

  5. Choose one. Not forever — you can change it up to the end of Week 4 by talking to me. But choose one now, because a decision made today gets four days of thinking that a decision made next Tuesday does not.

Photograph the wall before it comes down

All of it, including the ideas nobody took. It goes in your Component 1 as evidence of ideation, and it is the first thing to come back to if your topic collapses in Week 6.

Part six

The pitch gate

Ninety seconds each, to me, in front of whoever is still in the room. This carries no marks. It is a gate, not an assessment: the point is that nobody walks out of this session still deciding.

Fill in this sentence and say it out loud:

The pitch

“My piece is about [what], for [who], so that they understand or feel [what]. It works as a scroll because [which shape, and why down means something]. My evidence so far is [sources]. Somewhere around [roughly where], I think the reader should get to [do something].”

Then one line on what worries you about it. That line is the most useful thing you will say, and it is usually the thing I can fix on the spot.

What I am listening for, in order:

  • Does down mean something? If the sections could be shuffled, we work on that first.
  • Is it narrow enough? Nine pitches in ten are too big. “Climate change” is not a topic; one glacier, one lake, one street is.
  • Does the evidence exist? Not gathered yet — existing. Have you seen it with your own eyes?
  • Is there a place for the reader to act? Rough is fine. “Somewhere in the middle” is fine.

Three outcomes, all of them normal

Agreed — go and gather sources. Agreed, narrower — the most common outcome by a distance; you leave with the same subject and a smaller frame round it. Come back to me — usually an evidence problem, and usually resolved in a ten-minute conversation before you leave the building. Nobody is sent away with nothing.

Snapshot upload — before you leave

Week 3 is one of the three interim snapshot points. Upload to the MyBeckett snapshot box: your agreed topic in one sentence, your photograph of the wall, and the line you drew in Activity 1. Two minutes' work. It timestamps your ideation as your own, which is what the process-evidence trail in the brief is for.

Directed task · before Week 4

Gather your sources

Next week you storyboard. You cannot storyboard a story you have not researched, so this week's task is the research, and Week 4 visibly depends on it.

First, work through the research methods tutorial on the module site. It is a twenty-minute read covering what counts as a source at this level, how to check a claim, how to credit images you did not take, and how to keep a source list that will still make sense in January. It exists because a quarter of your Component 1 mark is research and verification, and every year somebody loses those marks for reasons that are entirely avoidable.

Bring to Week 4

  • Your topic in one sentence, in the pitch format above.
  • At least five credible sources, recorded properly — not five open tabs.
  • Twelve images or data points you are actually allowed to use, with where each came from. Rough and unedited is fine. Twelve sounds like a lot; it is roughly one per section, and you will discard half.
  • Your line, redrawn now you know more than you did today.
  • One thing that surprised you while researching. These are usually where the piece really starts.

If your topic falls apart during the week — the pictures do not exist, the story turns out not to be true — email me. Do not wait until next Monday to say so. A topic that collapses in Week 3 costs you nothing. One that collapses in Week 8 costs you a grade.

If you get stuck

I genuinely have no ideas

You have ideas; you have a filter set too high. Go back to Round C and write five things you know about, then show the list to somebody else and watch which one they ask a question about. That is the one. The filter you are applying — is this interesting enough, has this been done — belongs at the end of the process, not the start.

I have too many and cannot choose

Run the five tests honestly and most of them fall away. If two survive equally, pick the one with more pictures. Genuinely — at this level, available visual material is the difference between a piece you can finish and one you cannot.

Someone has already made my piece

Good. That means the story works and the material exists. Read theirs properly, write down the three decisions you would make differently, and make yours. Every piece on last week's watch-list has a dozen predecessors. Just do not copy the structure beat for beat, and do credit it if it shaped your thinking.

My topic is interesting but nothing changes

Then it wants to be a layered explainer rather than a change-over-time piece. The change becomes the change in the reader's understanding. That is a legitimate shape and the brief covers it explicitly — but it puts more weight on the reveal order, so plan for that in Week 4.

I cannot find images I am allowed to use

Check the licence before you fall in love with a picture. NASA and USGS material is generally free to reuse with credit; Our World in Data is CC BY. Museum and library archives vary enormously and many allow educational use if you ask. If a picture is not licensable, you can often photograph, draw, chart or reconstruct the same thing yourself, and the piece is usually better for it. The research methods tutorial covers this properly.

I want to change my topic after today

Allowed, up to the end of Week 4, by talking to me first. After that you are storyboarding and the cost of switching gets steep. Changing once is normal; changing three times is a different problem and worth a conversation.
