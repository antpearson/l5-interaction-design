Week 2

Before anything moves

Your site back on a new Mac, a layout and a scene that holds still, both built by you, and a long list of story ideas. No animation yet, on purpose.

Last week you built a page and put it on the internet. This week you find out what you might fill it with.

You might expect Week 2 to be the week things start moving. It is not, and that is a decision. Every year the same thing happens: someone gets a heading to fly in from the left, spends three weeks making more things fly in, and arrives at the first deadline with a lively page about nothing. Component 1 is marked entirely on your story, your audience and your concept. None of it is code.

So the animation library waits until Week 5, when you have a story for it to serve. Today does three things instead. It gets the Level 4 layout skills back under your fingers, on your own page. It shows you what scroll-driven pieces are made of, so your ideas are buildable ones. And it starts the ideas.

Before we start

You should have committed and synced your Week 1 work, and have a live GitHub Pages address. If you do not, or it shows a 404, sit near the front. We fix those first rather than leave you behind.

Part one · Task 1

Get your site back

Unless you are on your own laptop, you are probably not at the Mac you used last week, and the folder you made is not on this one. That is fine. Your site lives on GitHub; this Mac is just where you are working on it today. Getting it back is called cloning, and from now on it is how every studio session starts.

Task 1 · 15 minutes · on your own

Clone, then tell Git who you are

  1. Open VS Code. Close every tab it opens and ignore anything GitLens asks for.

  2. Click Clone Git Repository…, then Clone from GitHub. Sign in through the browser if it asks.

  3. Pick your-username/interaction-design from the list, choose the Desktop as the destination, and click Open.

  4. Open Terminal → New Terminal and run the two identity lines. This Mac has never met you.

    Terminal

    git config --global user.name "Your Name"
git config --global user.email "the-email-on-your-github-account"

  5. Right-click index.html and choose Open with Live Server. Your page from last week should be in front of you.

Part two · Task 2

Text beside a picture

A chapter in a scrolling story is almost always some text beside a picture, and it has to survive being 1400 pixels wide on the studio monitor and 390 on a phone. Your starter does not have one. You are going to build it, with the tool you used for exactly this last year: Flexbox.

First the HTML: a section with a class, holding a <div> of text and a <figure>.

index.html

<section class="chapter-figure">
  <div class="chapter-text">
    <h2>Your heading</h2>
    <p>…</p>
  </div>
  <figure>
    <img src="images/scene-01.svg" alt="Say what the picture shows.">
    <figcaption>What it shows, when, and who made it.</figcaption>
  </figure>
</section>

Then the CSS:

css/style.css

.chapter-figure {
  display: flex;
  flex-wrap: wrap;            /* drop to a new line when tight */
  gap: 3rem;                  /* space between them */
  align-items: center;        /* line them up vertically */
  max-width: 70rem;           /* wider than a text chapter */
  margin: 0 auto;
  padding: 4rem 2rem;
}
.chapter-figure .chapter-text { flex: 1 1 20rem; }
.chapter-figure figure        { flex: 1 1 24rem; margin: 0; }

The line worth understanding is flex: 1 1 20rem. It is three values at once: may grow, may shrink, aim for 20rem. The third number decides when the layout wraps, not a media query. Two children asking for 20rem and 24rem with a gap between them need about 48rem of room; below that, they stack. That is the whole responsive strategy for a chapter: no breakpoint, the content decides.

New this year: Grid

There is a second layout tool, CSS Grid, which you have not met yet. It does this same job with columns you name up front, and you will see it in other people's code. You do not need it today. When you are curious, MDN: Grid basics in the resources at the bottom of this page is the place to start.

Task 2 · 30 minutes · on your own page

Build a chapter with a picture

  1. In index.html, add a new <section> with the class chapter-figure, a <div class="chapter-text"> and a <figure>, as above. Use one of the placeholder pictures in images/. Save. The picture sits under the text, because nothing has told it otherwise.

  2. Before you look at the CSS above: on paper, from memory, write the Flexbox rule that would put two things side by side. Compare it with your neighbour's, then with the rule above.

  3. Type the rule into style.css, under YOUR STYLES. Type it, do not paste it. Save after each line and watch what each one does.

  4. Change 24rem to 40rem. Save and look. Put it back to something you like.

  5. Make the picture sit on the left. There is more than one way; find one. A hint: order, flex-direction, or swapping the two in the HTML.

  6. Drag your window from full width down to phone width. Nothing should overflow sideways. If it does, the last thing you changed did it.

  7. Commit, with a message that says what you built.

Part three · Task 3

The scene that holds still

A picture that stays put while boxes of text pass over it. That move, a picture that holds while the words change, is the single most recognisable thing in the whole genre. Snow Fall does it. Body on the Moor does it. It needs no JavaScript at all, and you are about to build it.

The markup is two siblings inside a section:

index.html

<section class="scene">
  <div class="scene-sticky">      <!-- the picture: this holds still -->
    <img src="images/scene-02.svg" alt="Say what the picture shows.">
  </div>
  <div class="scene-steps">       <!-- the words: these scroll past -->
    <div class="step"><p>…</p></div>
    <div class="step"><p>…</p></div>
    <div class="step"><p>…</p></div>
  </div>
</section>

The CSS that does the holding is three lines. The rest is Flexbox you already know, centring things:

css/style.css

.scene-sticky {
  position: sticky;   /* scroll normally, until... */
  top: 0;             /* ...you reach the top of the window. Then stay. */
  height: 100svh;     /* exactly one screen tall, like your cover */
  display: flex;
  align-items: center;
  justify-content: center;
}

.scene-steps {
  position: relative;       /* so the words pass in front of the picture */
  display: flex;
  flex-direction: column;
  align-items: center;
  gap: 60svh;               /* how far the reader scrolls between steps */
  padding-bottom: 40svh;
}

.step {
  max-width: 26rem;
  padding: 1rem 1.5rem;
  background: var(--paper); /* or the words vanish into the picture */
}

position: sticky means behave normally until you would scroll off, then stick. The picture stays stuck for as long as its parent, the .scene, is still on screen. The steps are what make the scene tall: each one is separated by gap: 60svh, a little over half a screen, so the reader has to scroll to reach the next. More steps, or bigger gaps, and the picture holds for longer. That gap is a pacing control, and it is the first one you have.

Task 3 · 35 minutes · on your own page

Build the scene, then tell something true in three steps

Not your assignment topic. Something small and true that you already know: how to make a proper cup of tea, the three stages of your commute, what happened to the pub at the end of your road. The point is to feel what it is like to write for this structure.

  1. Add the scene's HTML after your picture chapter. Save. It is a picture with three paragraphs under it. That is fine.

  2. Add the CSS, starting with .scene-sticky. Save after the first three lines and scroll: the picture already holds.

  3. Style the .step boxes so they belong to your page: your colours, your spacing.

  4. Write your three .step paragraphs so they tell your three-step story. One or two sentences each.

  5. Replace the placeholder picture with one that belongs to the story. Put it in images/, lower case, no spaces, and write a real alt.

  6. Add a fourth step. Notice that the picture now holds for longer, and you did not touch the CSS.

  7. In .scene-steps, change gap: 60svh to 120svh, then to 20svh. Scroll each. One drags and one rushes. Put it where your story reads best.

  8. Commit and sync. Check your live address updates.

What you just did

You made three decisions a reader will feel: what they see, the order the words arrive in, and how long they wait between them. That is pacing and reveal order, two of the four things from last week's definition, and there was no animation involved.

Part four · Screening

What these pieces are made of

You pitch a story next week, and it helps to know what is buildable before you fall for an idea. So this part is a screening, and for you it is watching only. I run some short demos on the projector, then we go back to the pieces from Week 1 and name what they are doing. You do not need to know how any of it is coded. That starts in Week 5, and by then you will know exactly which of these your story needs.

Almost everything in this genre is built from six moves:

The six moves. Most good pieces use three or four of them, not all six.
MoveWhat the reader seesWhat it is good for
Hold Something stays on screen while other things scroll past it. Keeping the subject in view while you talk about it. You built this one today.
Reveal Something arrives as the reader reaches it: fades up, slides in, draws itself. Directing attention. Used on everything, it directs attention nowhere.
Swap The held picture changes as each step of text arrives. Before and after. Then and now. The same place across forty years.
Scrub The scrollbar is wired directly to a change: a counter runs, a line draws, a map zooms. Scroll back and it runs backwards. Anything continuous. The engine of most change-over-time stories.
Layer Foreground and background move at different speeds. Depth and atmosphere. Easy to overdo, and the first thing to make people feel ill.
Respond The reader does something other than scroll, and the piece answers. The moment of genuine interaction the brief asks for. The other five are motion design.

Then we reopen three pieces from the Week 1 screening list and call the moves out as they happen. You will find you can name nearly everything on screen, and that is a different way of reading: last week you were the audience, this week you can see the joins.

Keep this table next to you in Part five

When an idea turns up, ask which of the six it would lean on. “The high street, then and now” is a swap. “How deep the mine goes” is a scrub. If an idea needs none of them, it may be an article rather than a scroll story. If it needs something that is not on the list, tell me, because that is either a problem or the best idea in the room.

Part five · Activity

Ideas, and lots of them

The brief asks for a true story or an explainer: something that has changed, developed or run down over time, or a subject your reader comes to understand step by step. The topic is yours. Next week you choose one. This week the job is the opposite of choosing.

People who say they have no ideas usually have three, have already decided all three are rubbish, and have stopped. The way round that is volume. The first ideas anyone has are the ones everyone has. The interesting ones turn up somewhere after number ten, once the obvious is used up and you are slightly bored.

Activity · 40 minutes · laptops shut, paper out

Thirty ideas before you leave

Two rules. Nobody judges anything today, including their own. And every idea gets written down, in a few words, however thin it seems.

  1. Round one: what you already know. 10 minutes, on your own. Five things you know more about than most people in this room: a place, a job, a sport, a scene, a family story, an obsession. For each, write two things about it that have changed, or that outsiders always get wrong. That is ten ideas.

  2. Round two: prompts. 10 minutes, on your own. Two answers to each, fast. The second answer is usually better than the first.

    • Something that used to be everywhere and now is not.
    • A building near me that has had at least three different lives.
    • A job that has nearly disappeared in my lifetime.
    • Something everyone thinks they understand and almost nobody does.
    • Something my grandparents would not recognise about where I grew up.
    • A thing I end up explaining every time it comes up.
    • Somewhere I can walk to that has a story attached.
    • Something that got better, quietly, and nobody made a fuss.

  3. Round three: swap. 10 minutes, in pairs. Swap sheets. Read your partner's list and add one idea underneath any three of theirs: a twist, a narrower version, the same thing somewhere else. Then swap back and read what they did to yours.

  4. Round four: mark, do not choose. 10 minutes. Put a star by the five you would most like to read if somebody else made them. Next to each star, write which of the six moves it might lean on. Do not pick a winner. That is next week.

Keep the sheet

Photograph it before you leave and bring it next week. It is the raw material for the Week 3 workshop, and the first page of your research trail for Component 1.

If you get stuck

My repository is not in the clone list

Either you are signed in to GitHub as somebody else (shared Macs remember the last person; sign out on github.com and start again), or last week's publish never finished. Check github.com in the browser. If the repository is not there, it was never published: see Part 1 of Working with GitHub.

“Make sure you configure your user.name and user.email”

You skipped step 4 of Task 1. None of the buttons on that message help. Click Cancel, run the two identity lines in the terminal, then commit again.

The picture in my scene does not hold still

Three usual causes. A parent element has overflow: hidden, which switches sticky off without telling you. The sticky element has lost its top: 0. Or you have moved the steps outside the .scene section, so the scene is only one screen tall and there is nothing to hold through.

My page scrolls sideways on a narrow window

Something is wider than the screen. Usually an image without max-width: 100%, or a fixed width in pixels that you added. Open DevTools, turn on the device toolbar, and look for the element poking out of the right-hand edge.

I changed the CSS and nothing happened

Check the file is saved (a dot on the tab means it is not). Check you are looking at the Live Server tab, not a copy you opened by double-clicking. Then check whether the rule sits inside the @media (min-width: 60rem) block, and if it does, that your window is actually that wide.

I cannot think of any ideas

Nobody can, on demand, in a quiet room. Go back to round one and make the list of things you know about longer and more boring: your street, your part-time job, the bus you catch. Boring subjects make good pieces because nobody has looked at them properly.
