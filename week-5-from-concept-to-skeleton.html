Week 5

Pulling Component 1 together

Component 1 is due on Friday. Today is about working on the documentation and getting it ready.

I am not going to take you through the brief again; it is quoted below if you need it.

We are going to work in short sprints in this class. By the end of the session, you will have a PDF with all six items in it, however thin some of them are. The time between now and Friday is for filling gaps on those thinner items.

I will be going round all session, and I will start with whoever is furthest behind.

The deadline

Component 1 (Research & Concept, 30%) is uploaded to MyBeckett by Friday 30 October at 12 noon.

From the assignment brief

What Component 1 asks for

One PDF, uploaded to MyBeckett by Friday 30 October, 12 noon, with your AI-use log as a separate file. Six things, in this order.

  1. Story research: your verified story or topic, with credible sources showing what is true and how you know
  2. Key influences: the interactive narratives and other work that have shaped your thinking, and what you are taking from each
  3. Audience and purpose: who the piece is for and what you want them to understand or feel
  4. Your concept: premise and experience arc. What does your reader encounter, understand and do from the top of the page to the end? pitch swap
  5. Moodboard and concept sketches: the visual direction of the piece (imagery, type, colour, tone) and early sketches of how it might look
  6. Interaction plan: a motion storyboard or annotated sketch of the scroll sections showing pacing beats, key transitions and at least one designed moment of genuine interaction sprints 1 and 2

Four marking criteria, 25% each: research and influences; audience and purpose; concept and visual direction; interaction plan. The wording above is the brief's own. If this page and the brief on MyBeckett ever disagree, the brief wins.

What to have with you

Everything you have, whatever state it is in: your beat sheet, storyboard frames, moodboard, research notes and any draft of the document. Paper, a pen you can draw with, and your laptop. If you run out of frames, the storyboard kit prints onto A4.

Part one

Where are you?

Before anything else, let’s find out where everyone is up to.

Rate your six items

  1. Open the start-of-session check-in. It lists the six items from the brief, and your name is not recorded.
  2. Lay out everything you have and rate each item. Green: you could hand it in as it is. Amber: started, but thin. Red: not there yet.
  3. Submit. I put the results on the screen: they show me where the room is and where I should go first.
  4. Make a list of your reds and ambers. Put your worst first. This is the order to focus on today.

One thing about the marking is worth repeating. The six items are marked on four criteria, each worth a quarter of the component, so fifteen pages of research and half a page of storyboard earns marks on one criterion and very few on another. Balance the document the way the marks are balanced. If you put green for any of the six items, see if the item meets the criteria of the right-hand column below.

Which items feed which criterion. Each criterion is 25% of Component 1.
Criterion Fed by The top band wants
Research the story and its influences Story research; key influences Every claim verified against credible sources, research that visibly shapes the piece, and influences you can say something specific about.
Define the audience and purpose Audience and purpose Audience precisely defined; purpose clear enough that it drives every later design decision.
Develop a concept and visual direction for a scroll-driven narrative Your concept; moodboard and concept sketches A concept that could only work as a scrolling narrative, and a visual direction you could build from.
Plan the interaction in a motion storyboard Interaction plan Pacing beats annotated, every decision justified against the story, at least one designed moment of choice.

Part two

Pitch swap

Before you spend the session drawing, check that what you are drawing makes sense to someone who is not you.

Pitch swap · about 20 minutes · in pairs

Pitch it, then listen

  1. Sixty seconds. Pitch your piece: what it is about, who it is for, and what the reader knows by the end. You do not need a storyboard for this. Do not apologise for what is not finished.
  2. Your partner reads your beat sheet and points to the first place the order stopped making sense. Write that down. If you have no beat sheet with you, talk them through the order instead.
  3. Swap.
  4. Five minutes on your own. Write the “Your concept” section of the document from your pitch, and fix the place where your partner got lost.

Keep the notes

Where your partner got lost, and what you changed because of it, are the first entry in your iteration trail. Component 2 asks for exactly that, and it starts here.

Part three

Three sprints

The rest of the session is three timed sprints, each with one job. When the time is up, move on, even if the job is not finished. A whole document with thin patches is worth more than half a perfect one.

Sprint 1 · 35 minutes · on your own

A frame for every beat

Rough and complete beats beautiful and half done. The criterion is about communicating the whole piece, so twelve scruffy frames mark better than five beautiful ones and a gap.

  1. Count the beats on your beat sheet, then count your frames. The difference is this sprint's job.
  2. Draw the missing frames first, in order, before you improve a single existing one. Boxes, arrows and squiggles for text are enough. I am not marking your draughtsmanship.
  3. Annotate each frame with the notation from last week and its scroll distance in screen heights.
  4. Then go back and add a line under each frame on why. Annotations that say what happens are worth a pass. Annotations that say why this movement serves this moment are worth a first.
  5. A beat you are unsure about can say so. A note reading “undecided between these two” is design thinking, not a hole.

Storyboard already complete?

Work on the worst item on your list from Part one instead. If that is your research, make sure it says what you found that changed your plan: the top band asks for research that visibly shapes the piece.

Break

Sprint 2 · 25 minutes · on your own, then with someone else

Your interaction moment

The interaction plan is a quarter of the marks, and the brief asks for at least one designed moment of genuine interaction: somewhere the reader does something other than scroll. A sentence describing it is not enough. Draw it.

  1. Pick the moment. If you have more than one, pick the one the story needs most.
  2. Give it a whole page: one large frame, or two or three if there is a before and an after.
  3. Annotate what the reader does, what changes in response, and what they see if they do nothing at all.
  4. Write the why: why this moment in the story, and why it needs the reader's hand rather than plain scrolling.
  5. Show another member of the class your interaction and ask them if the interaction is intuitive and easy to do.

Sprint 3 · 30 minutes · on your own

Assemble the PDF

You should leave this class with a document that exists and can be exported as a PDF. It does not need to be good yet, but it needs to contain all six sections so that these can be embellished by the hand-in.

  1. Open the document in whatever you are making it in: Word, PowerPoint, Google Docs, InDesign, Canva, anything that exports a PDF. Put in the six headings, in the brief's order.
  2. Put in everything you have. Photograph or scan paper work flat, in good light. Make the storyboard legible at full width rather than four frames to a page.
  3. Where a section is thin, write TO DO under the heading and say what is missing. A gap you can see is a gap you will fill.
  4. Put a credit next to every image you did not make. Do it now, while you remember where they came from.
  5. Try exporting the PDF and make sure that the photos/scans of your storyboard are clear enough to be read. Don't be afraid of using multiple pages for your storyboard to make it easier to read.

Part four

Before you go

About 10 minutes · on your own

Rate them again

Go back to your list from Part one, rate the six items again, and put your new ratings into the end-of-session check-in. Whatever is still red or amber is your plan for the next three days, worst first.

Your AI-use log

Component 1 is two files, uploaded together through the MyBeckett link: your PDF and your AI-use log. The log is ai-use-log.docx in your starter project, or you can download a fresh copy.

  • Add a row for every time you used an AI tool for anything on this module: the date, the tool, what you asked it for, what you did with the result, and where it is in your work.
  • Using it for ideas, or to explain an error, is fine. Your story text and code must be your own, and any AI-made image must be listed.
  • If you have not used AI, upload the log anyway and say so.

If something goes wrong on Friday morning, upload what you have before noon and email me. A slightly incomplete submission on time is an entirely different situation from a late one.

If you get stuck

I have almost nothing

Then today matters more for you than for anyone, and it is still enough time to change that. Be honest in the check-in in Part one, so I know to come to you first. Do the pitch swap: your concept section comes out of it. Then do the sprints in order and do not skip Sprint 3. A thin document on Friday is a completely different situation from no document.

The pitch swap made me want to change everything

Normal, and usually an overreaction. Change the order and the opening if they need it; both are cheap. Do not change your topic this close to the hand-in. Write the bigger doubts down; some of them will turn out to be the iteration evidence Component 2 wants.

How long should the document be?

There is no word count, but as a rough shape:

  • Story research: two or three pages, with your sources listed and what each one told you.
  • Key influences: a page or two. Three or four pieces, each with a screenshot and what you are taking from it.
  • Audience and purpose: about a page. Who your reader is, what they already know, and what you want them to understand or feel by the end.
  • Your concept: a page or two. The premise, and the experience arc from the top of the page to the end.
  • Moodboard and concept sketches: two or three pages, with a credit under every picture you did not make.
  • Interaction plan: the storyboard, over as many pages as it needs to stay readable.

If your storyboard is not the longest part, the balance is probably wrong.

Can I still change my mind about the interaction moment?

Yes. Component 1 asks you to have designed one, not to be bound to it for ever. Changing it later with a reason is iteration; never having designed one is a missing criterion.
